The Courage to Commit
Shawn Johnson & Andrew East

You've probably felt the restlessness of a life lived in perpetual maybe. Every time you start to go deep into something, a voice whispers that you're missing out on what else might be out there – so you pull back, hedge, and end up mediocre at a dozen things instead of exceptional at one.
The result is a strange kind of drift: busy but not progressing, interested but never committed, always rehearsing and never performing. In this Blink, you'll discover the counterintuitive strength of narrowing your focus and choosing deliberate, unwavering dedication. You'll learn how to silence the low-grade anxiety of choice overload, handle inevitable setbacks with grace, and design daily rhythms that actually stick. By the end, you'll walk away as someone who no longer drifts through a cycle of temporary fixes – but instead carries the steady confidence of a person who knows how to build a deep, skilled, and deeply meaningful life.
Modern life floods you with options. Swipe left, swipe right, keep scrolling – the promise is that more choice equals more freedom. And yet, all that abundance tends to breed a low-grade, persistent anxiety. You're trained to keep doors open, to delay committing, to always have a Plan B warming up on the bench.
But here's the strange part: your body actually registers too many options as a threat. When you're staring down a massive array of possibilities, your cardiovascular system kicks into overdrive – your heart pumps harder, blood flow shifts, and your physiology starts mimicking someone who feels genuinely incapable of choosing well. Backup plans sound smart in theory, but in practice they split your attention, drain your mental reserves, and signal doubt to everyone watching. A case in point is that of co-author Andrew East when he was a top-rated university athlete heading into the professional football draft. Multiple teams were interested, so it seemed like he was sitting pretty. But he was also taking venture capital meetings and entertaining an offer to play in a rugby tournament in Trinidad and Tobago.
During one off-season camp, he tried to straddle two worlds – holding 111 kilograms of muscle for football while simultaneously training for a marathon. At mile 16, his body shut down completely. He needed two intravenous drips just to rehydrate. He limped into the pro team facility underweight, cramping through his physicals, and arrived at his dream job in the worst shape of his life. He thought he was being strategic. Coaches saw something different: divided attention.
Nine teams ended up cutting him. Partial dedication, it turns out, is impossible to hide. Now flip that picture. Deep dedication works like a protective ecosystem, because one overarching commitment wipes out thousands of tiny daily decisions. That’s what happened to co-author Shawn Johnson East while preparing for the Olympics. Every single day followed the same rhythm – 5:00 a.
m. wake-up, morning practice, recovery, afternoon training, film study. Walking into a grocery store, entire aisles simply blurred into irrelevance. The snack aisle, the ice cream freezers – none of it registered. The decision had already been made. That's the real gift of going all in.
Every choice you face throughout a day – what to eat, what to wear, how to respond to an email – draws from the same finite pool of mental energy. When your path is already decided, a thousand smaller decisions just vanish. Your mental reserves stay intact for the work that actually matters. The heavy weight of infinite possibility lifts and what replaces it is a startling sense of calm. And that calm runs straight into the friction of actually doing the work – the long, unglamorous road to getting good at something. In the next section, you’ll see how.
Setting out on a long, repetitive road toward expertise comes with a surprising truth about how progress actually works. You'd expect a smooth, upward climb once you commit to a new skill. The reality follows a pattern called a J-curve, and it can be deeply frustrating. At first, everything clicks – the basics arrive fast, confidence builds, and optimism feels justified.
Then the path drops sharply. Performance dips, even as effort increases. What felt instinctive days ago now feels clumsy. Your brain and body are busy reorganizing themselves around deeper principles. That temporary regression is a signal that real integration is underway. Most casual enthusiasts quit right at this dip.
They assume they've hit a natural ceiling, so they chase the next fresh pursuit for another hit of beginner's momentum. The discomfort of the valley is exactly what they're trying to avoid. And yet, for Shawn, the distance between crying through her first forward roll as a terrified three-year-old and executing a double-twisting double back as an elite Olympian came down to hundreds of thousands of repetitions. Elite performers learn to welcome the dip. They develop a kind of connoisseur's taste for different setbacks, treating each one as specific, useful data – never as a final verdict on their ability. Surviving that dip means crossing a vast stretch of sameness.
The human brain evolved to scan constantly for novelty – fresh signals that might mean opportunity or danger. When a task offers nothing new, dopamine pathways get restless and attention drifts. This creates a real tension between a culture built around constant stimulation and the grinding reality of daily practice. To reach the Olympic podium, Shawn had to push through this exact monotony, logging over 16,000 hours of identical drills until her hands were raw – all for roughly 16 hours of actual Olympic competition. That mind-numbing repetition works as a filter, separating the truly committed from the merely curious. When Andrew was playing collegiate football, he wore the same unwashed yellow training shirt for an entire year.
By December it was stiff with salt stains, practically standing on its own. Pulling it on each morning physically reinforced his identity and dedication. He noticed that everyone around him pretended to be surprised by how boring the agility drills were – when the gap between game-day chaos and daily monotony was the whole point. Naming that boredom out loud makes the process far easier to digest. Pushing through that stretch of tedium opens the door to extraordinary focus. Master craftspeople often slip into transcendent flow states right after long stretches of mechanical repetition.
Once you stop chasing novelty, you develop a still, steady gaze and a deep physical presence – shifting from watching the clock to noticing the fine details of the work itself. Raw determination alone, though, will drain your reserves at that level of monotony. A more systematic approach is what keeps you moving forward.
Trying to push through the exhausting middle stretch of any big goal on raw determination alone sounds admirable, but the strategy falls apart fast. Willpower works a lot like a phone battery – it drains steadily as the day goes on and tends to die at the worst possible moment. A far more reliable approach is to shape your surroundings so they do the work for you. Research shows that roughly 43 percent of everything you do in a day runs on autopilot – habitual responses triggered by cues in your environment.
A coffee maker sitting on the counter kicks off your morning routine without a second thought. A fruit bowl on the dining table wins out over cookies tucked away in the pantry. And people who live close to a grocery store with a big produce section eat more fruits and vegetables, no matter what they say about their diet. Convenience beats conviction, every single time. So, your surroundings are constantly steering your behavior, whether you realize it or not. And clutter makes everything harder.
A messy desk, piles of unsorted mail, surfaces covered in random stuff – all of that acts like a slow tax on your attention. It chips away at the same mental energy you need to stay focused on the thing that actually matters to you. Clearing the physical space gives your mind room to breathe. That brings us to a practical question: How do you actually set this up? Well, you become the architect of your own defaults. You arrange things so the right choice is always the easiest one.
Shawn and Andrew found that they kept spending too much money at coffee shops and missing their mornings together. So they bought a high-end espresso machine and placed it right in the kitchen. That one adjustment removed the friction of leaving the house, and a simple morning coffee became a daily ritual of connection – no discipline required. Their surroundings made the desired behavior the path of least resistance. You can apply this same logic anywhere. If your guitar is buried in a case at the back of a closet, requiring six separate steps before you can play a single chord, practice will fade.
But leave your gym bag packed and visible by the front door, and the decision practically makes itself. Once your space is set up to support your goals, something interesting happens – the question shifts from “How do I get started? " to "How do I keep going? " Your environment has laid the groundwork. Now the next step is learning to track the invisible progress building beneath the surface.
So, how do you actually see progress when the results haven't shown up yet? You track it. Decades of behavioral psychology point to something striking: the simple act of paying attention to your own efforts reshapes how you relate to your goals. Tracking creates a feedback loop between your daily actions and the bigger picture you're working toward.
And here's what makes this counterintuitive – when the numbers disappoint you, that gap between where you are and where you want to be becomes a source of fuel. The disappointment itself sharpens your motivation to adapt. Now, the kind of thing you measure makes all the difference. Tracking daily actions you can control – the stuff entirely in your hands – leads to dramatically better outcomes than fixating on results you can't influence. An aspiring writer who logs pages written each day will stick with the craft far longer than one who only watches for acceptance letters. Counting workouts completed builds a sense of agency that stepping on a bathroom scale never will.
Process metrics keep you in the driver's seat, and over time, the accumulated evidence of your own consistency becomes its own kind of confidence. You end up racing against yesterday's version of yourself, turning ordinary daily habits into an engaging game. This same principle stretches into the softer areas of life – like love, connection, or joy. Andrew’s late father implemented this into his life by turning a 19-minute morning commute into a daily adventure, all by timing tandem bike rides to school with his kids. He brought that same attention to everything he cared about, logging how many nights his family spent at their lake house and eventually building a 473-day streak of writing daily devotionals for his clients. His simple tallies turned abstract intentions into something concrete and visible.
Shawn and Andrew took the same approach after leaving professional sports. They started tracking date nights, family dinners, and thank-you texts sent to friends. The data worked like an early-warning system. During one particularly busy stretch, they discovered they'd spent zero evenings together as a family of five – despite genuinely believing family time was their top priority.
That's the thing about tracking soft variables: it forces you to define what you actually value, then hold yourself accountable to it. The best way to begin is small – pick one controllable action, record it daily, and watch the streak build. Of course, tracking your metrics guarantees something else too: you’ll eventually watch yourself fall short. And that leads us to the final piece of this puzzle – learning how to handle failure without walking away entirely.
When you track your goals and fall short, you come face to face with a version of yourself you'd rather avoid. A missed target kicks off a brutal inner monologue – and most people slip into the role of their own worst critic, turning one skipped workout into proof that they're fundamentally lazy. That kind of thinking feeds directly into what psychologists call learned helplessness. Once that defeated attitude takes hold, you start building elaborate excuses and quietly undermining your own progress.
The very identity you're working to leave behind starts tightening its grip. So, how do you break that cycle? The answer is grace. Self-compassion – the clinical term for giving yourself grace – turns out to be one of the most effective psychological tools available. Researchers who put people through self-compassion training found that participants performed better, stuck with hard tasks longer, and bounced back from setbacks much faster. In one study, people who responded to a failed test with kindness spent significantly more time preparing for a retest.
The self-critical group? They avoided studying altogether – revisiting their mistakes was simply too painful. Treating yourself with the same warmth you'd offer a struggling friend produces measurably better outcomes. A great example of this grace-filled mindset in action comes from Shawn who hit the mat over 50,000 times during her career. Falling off the balance beam was a constant. Her coach treated every fall as completely ordinary.
After a spectacular wipeout, he'd cheerfully point out that she now knew exactly how that mistake felt in her body. Pain became tuition. Frustration became the expected cost of showing up. She reframed each fall as useful data – one repetition closer to getting it right. That shift in perspective kept her from ever abandoning the process entirely. And you can build this kind of resilience yourself.
One practical method is the friend test: when you break a commitment, ask yourself what you'd say to a close friend in that same spot, then direct that grace inward. Another approach is simply zooming out – picturing yourself five years from now. Most of today's catastrophes shrink into minor footnotes from that vantage point. Even the language you use matters here.
Swapping "I failed at this" for "I am learning this" rewires how your brain responds to difficulty and keeps your momentum alive. When you finally let grace break that cycle of self-criticism, your whole relationship with commitment shifts. You stop treating yourself like an opponent and start treating yourself like someone worth investing in. Showing up for your goals feels less like a grind and more like an act of genuine self-respect – because you're finally backing someone you believe in.
In this Blink to The Courage to Commit by Shawn Johnson and Andrew East, you’ve learned that true skill and deep meaning emerge when you strip away endless options and fully commit to the rigorous, repetitive demands of a singular path.   The road to expertise includes long stretches of monotony that actively test your resolve. By redesigning your physical environment to remove daily friction, you make your desired behaviors the path of least resistance. Tracking your daily process creates a reliable feedback loop that keeps you moving forward even when motivation fades.
  A firm system of self-compassion accelerates your progress, helping you treat inevitable setbacks as useful data. And removing your escape routes – staying in the room during the hardest periods – opens up a level of clarity and capability that stays out of reach for those who constantly pivot.
