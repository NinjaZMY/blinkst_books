Visual Intelligence
Amy E. Herman


Have you ever thought about how often your safety is reliant on Someone else's eyes?

Whether it’s boarding a flight, riding in a taxi or being wheeled into surgery, you're trusting a stranger's ability to notice what matters.

This might not always be a matter of life or death, but it can still shape whether you're promoted or held back, or whether your day stays run-of-the-mill or becomes one you’ll never forget.

Observing well and communicating what you’ve seen are crucial skills, but many people don’t make the most of them.

The good news is that we also have the innate ability to do this well.

What we lack, however, is the framework and the training to do so effectively.  

In this Blink, you'll discover how to sharpen your observation and communication abilities using four skills called the four As.

These are assessing what's around you, analyzing it from multiple angles, articulating what you find with precision and adapting when the situation stays uncertain.

Let’s get started.

The entrepreneur and humanitarian Derreck Kayongo once noticed something in a hotel room that millions of travelers before him had overlooked.

He observed that a fresh bar of soap had been swapped in for the barely touched one he'd left the night before.

Curious, he went down to the front desk to ask what had happened to the old bar.  

The answer stunned him.

He discovered that staff replaced the soap in every room, every day, and dropped the used bars straight into the trash.

Kayongo, who'd grown up without steady access to soap, couldn't get the thought out of his head.

He decided that something should be done, and began collecting, disinfecting and redistributing hotel soap to communities that needed it.

Eventually he built this into a charity that’s reached people across dozens of countries.

Kayongo’s actions weren’t the result of special powers.

He just noticed something everyone else walked past.

He noticed what matters.

The good news is that anyone can do this.

The ability to see what matters isn't rare or fixed.

Your brain processes far more than your eyes ever take in, and with the right training, you can teach it to notice more and interpret what it finds more accurately.

So, let’s start now with this training and the first of our four skills.

It’s learning to Assess what's in front of you before you do anything else with it.

Assessing well starts with knowing the difference between seeing and observing.

Seeing happens automatically, whether you're paying attention or not.

Observing means noticing consciously, with intent.

A classic example of this comes from the work of Arthur Conan Doyle.

In one story, Sherlock Holmes makes the distinction to Dr.

Watson by pointing out that Watson had gone up his staircase more times than he could count, yet never registered how many steps it had.

In other words, he'd seen them but he hadn't observed them.

The gap between observing and seeing exists because your brain can't process everything in front of you at once.

Faced with this overwhelming amount of data, it filters out whatever it decides isn't relevant.

Psychologists call this inattentional blindness, and you can see it everywhere.

For example, in one famous video, people are asked to count how many times a group of people pass a basketball.

As they intently focus only on the ‘relevant’ information, around half will fail to notice a person in a gorilla suit walk straight through the middle of the action, even when the gorilla stops to beat its chest.

Radiologists searching lung scans for cancerous nodules do no better; in one study when they’re shown a scan with a small gorilla image hidden inside it, most miss that too.

None of this means your eyes – or indeed, your brain – are broken.

It means observation, like any skill, has to be practiced deliberately before it becomes automatic.

But noticing accurately is only half the job of assessing well.

Two people standing side by side can look at the exact same scene and walk away with two entirely different accounts of what happened.

Let’s find out how in the next chapter.

The psychologist Samuel Renshaw liked to repeat the same little experiment.

He would show a blurry, poorly printed black-and-white photograph to visitors to his lab and ask them a simple question: What do you see?

Nearly every adult would get it wrong, yet every child Renshaw showed it to named the image correctly and immediately.

The image was a photograph of a cow.

Adults were generally only able to see the animal after someone had traced an outline around the animal's face.

After that, there was no unseeing it.

Adults and children, shaped by the experiences they had of filtering the world, would see two entirely different things in the exact same photograph.

This effect doesn’t just happen between children and adults – and it isn’t limited to image interpretation either.

We all see the world and what happens to us in different ways.  

Now, you might understand how this can apply to strangers; people who know nothing about each other and who might have completely different filters.

But you might be surprised to learn that it also applies to people who are extremely close.

For example, one married couple who escaped the same building together on 9/11 gave accounts of what they noticed and how they felt that morning that were so different, that they barely sounded like the same event.

So if no one sees the same thing in the same way, whose impression do you trust – your own or someone else’s?

There’s a simple habit that can help you make the best call.

Look at something and form your own view before anyone tells you what it's supposed to be, or what somebody else thinks of it.

Only when you’ve done this should you take in outside opinions or labels and take a second look.

You do this because looking first protects you from seeing only what you were told to see.

Next, let’s look at how you turn your own observations into something usable.

To do this effectively, you can turn to an approach used by journalists, investigators and researchers called who, what, when and where.

To help us understand this approach, let’s apply it to Edward Hopper's 1927 painting Automat, which shows a woman sitting alone with a cup of coffee.

If you use the who, what, when and where approach, you’ll notice the little details that help you build up a better observation of the scene.

You won’t just see a woman with a cup of coffee; you’ll likely build up a picture of her probable age, her mood and even the decade she’s in, purely from what's visible on the canvas.  

Another approach to refining your observation is to sharpen your descriptions; the sharper and more descriptive they are, the more useful they become.

For example, swap out vague words for numbers wherever you can.

So, instead of saying a room has “a few chairs,” count them.

Or instead of calling something “small,” measure it instead.

This precision will turn a vague impression into a fact that you or someone else can act on.

But even with the careful, factual approach we’ve outlined above, you can still miss what's directly in front of you.

For example, the author once watched two doctors examine a patient and talk loudly about his case while his wife sat right beside him.

They had no idea she was even there until she finally spoke up and said something.

One great way to catch what hides in plain sight like this is to try the COBRA method – that’s Camouflage, One, Break, Realign, Ask.

To use this method, look for whatever's Camouflaged rather than what stands out, focus on One thing at a time instead of skimming everything at once, take a Break if you're staring too hard, Realign your expectations about what you think you should be seeing, and finally Ask someone else to take a look, too.

Okay, now you know how to assess more carefully.

But once you've gathered accurate facts, you still have to decide which matter most.

That's where our second skill comes in: analyzing what you've found.

For years, the world looked away from Morro da Providência, a hillside slum in Rio de Janeiro.

They didn’t even look when three young men were murdered by soldiers and left in a trash dump.

Reporters wouldn't even climb the hill to cover the story.  

But then something changed.

In the middle of one night, the favela's walls were covered in giant black-and-white photographs of human eyes, staring out at the city below.

The images were the work of an artist known only as JR, who’d spent a month living among its residents, photographing them up close.

Naturally, the images finally got people looking toward the area; the media finally bothered climbing the hill to interview whoever had painted them.

But JR had vanished.

With no artist to talk to, the reporters were forced to talk to local people instead.

Thanks in part to this dialogue, the story shifted from violence and blame to the humanity of the people living there; within six years, the city had installed a cable car connecting the favela to the world below.

What happened to make this change possible?

JR didn't change what was on that hill; he changed the angle from which everyone else saw it.

That's our second skill for sharpening perception and communication: once you've assessed and gathered accurate facts, you have to Analyze them from every angle before trusting your conclusions.  

Many of us tend to look at the world from a fixed viewpoint, but this only shows part of the picture.

Just take Michelangelo's David. 

From the ground – the angle almost everyone sees him from – he looks calm and assured.

But climb a little higher and see him face-to-face, as it were, and you see flared nostrils and a furrowed brow – someone tense with fear.

Moving around something, above it, below it, even upside down, can reveal a different reality far more than a single glance ever would.

The same goes for people.

If you step into someone else's position and ask what the world looks like from their perspective, you’ll often understand behavior that looks baffling from your own viewpoint.

However, seeing things from every angle isn’t the last step.

You have the relevant information; you still have to decide what matters most.

To do this effectively, ask yourself three questions: What do I know?

What don't I know?

What do I need to know?  

The middle question – what don’t I know?

– is the one that a lot of people skip.

But absence can often tell you just as much as presence.

Let’s return to that master of observation, Sherlock Holmes.

In one of his cases, The Adventure of Silver Blaze, the key clue is a guard dog that doesn’t bark during a theft.

Holmes realizes that the absence of barking could only make sense if the dog knew the culprit.

The whole case was solved by what wasn’t there.

Noticing what should be there but isn't is often the fastest route to the truth.

Deciding which facts matter also means telling urgency apart from importance.

An urgent problem is one that screams for attention in the moment, but which only really matters in the short term.

An important one pays off over the long run, even if it feels less pressing in the moment.

If you don't remember this – and so many of us don’t – you may end up solving the problems that don’t matter, just because they’re the loudest.

So before you act, ask which category of problem you're really dealing with, and don’t just rush to the most urgent issue.

Now, assessing and analyzing facts won’t help others if they can’t understand what you've found.

So we’ll turn to communication next.

In May 2001, federal intern Chandra Levy vanished in Washington, DC.

Investigators traced the last entries in her search history to a large park and ordered a sweep a hundred yards on either side of its trails.

But somewhere along the chain of command, the word “trails” was swapped for the word “roads.”

The search team scoured along the park’s roads and found nothing.

Only ten months later did they find Chandra Levy’s body, seventy-nine yards off a trail.

By that point, most of the forensic evidence that could have identified her killer had disappeared.

This example is a brutal reminder that noticing and weighing facts carefully isn't enough if you can't communicate them accurately to someone else.

This is our third vital skill: Articulating what you've found so precisely that nobody has to guess what you mean.

Here are a few methods that can help you do this.

The first is to choose objective words over subjective ones.

For example, imagine a teacher who tells a review committee that a student is "lazy" and "difficult," but can’t name a single specific behavior to back it up.

The teacher’s words are opinions, not facts.

To avoid this, swap judgment words for observable ones – instead of "he's lazy," say "he didn't turn in three assignments this week."

Specificity matters too.

The businessman Joe Lentini once asked a waitress in a restaurant the price of a bottle of wine and was told “thirty-seven fifty.”

He heard $37.50 – wouldn’t you?!

He was in for the surprise of his life when the bill came to $3,750.

This simple confusion would have been easily avoided if the waitress had stated the full number when he asked.

Precision in communication also requires you to state plain facts even when they're uncomfortable.

Faced with something tricky to say, people often reach for euphemisms.

For instance, when viewing a painting of a nude, overweight woman, most of us might say she’s naked, but how many would say she’s overweight or obese?

Neither word is a judgment; both are clinically accurate descriptions, but would you use them?

The same discomfort that makes people dance around what they see in a painting can make them blind to a person's injuries.

In one tragic case from the UK, a young boy showed repeated signs of starvation and abuse, yet the adults around him documented what they saw without ever calling it what it was – they were more comfortable searching for seemingly rational reasons for his injuries than accepting the distressing truth.

In this case, their failure to say anything had fatal results.

Now, attention to detail and honesty are all well and good.

But what do you do when the person you're communicating with takes things the wrong way?

What do you do if the conversation gets heated?

In this case, you need to Repeat, Rename and Reframe.

Start by repeating the other person's point in your own words, so they know they've been heard.

If blame is muddying the issue, Rename the whole mess simply as a miscommunication, since then nobody has to defend themselves against that fact.

And if you're tempted to make an accusation, Reframe it as a question instead, asking what happened rather than declaring what went wrong.

Getting the words right still depends on knowing who's listening.

That's our fourth and final skill: learning to adapt.

A New York tabloid once ran the headline “NURSING HOME STRIPPER SCANDAL.”

The story painted a lurid picture of elder abuse accompanied by the image of a muscular stripper looming over a confused, vulnerable woman in a wheelchair.

People were horrified.

But when investigator Lucy Agate looked into things, she found something else entirely.

The nursing home residents had voted for the entertainment themselves and had tipped the performers with their own money.

They also had the option to stay out of the action.

In fact, their only complaint afterward was that the stripper was terrible.

Nevertheless, the story had exploded nationally because it fit a bias nobody wanted to admit: we don't expect elderly people in wheelchairs to have sexual desires of their own.

Everyone carries biases like this, whether they're your own or someone else's, and they can cause real harm when left unchecked.

There are examples of these biases everywhere.

One man, named José Zamora, faced a different kind of bias entirely.

After months of sending out résumés with no response, he decided to change his name by just one letter; he dropped the “s” from “José” to make it “Joe.”

Soon after, the interview requests started pouring in.  

José’s case shows bias in action, but it also shows one of the ways to counteract it.

Because he couldn't correct every employer's bias himself, he instead adapted around it.

This represents one part of the fourth and final skill for more effective perception and communication.

You must Adapt what you've assessed, analyzed and articulated to a world that won't always sit still and cooperate.  

The other element of adapting is knowing how to act best when a situation remains unresolved.

A great example of this comes from the shocking events in 1982, when seven people in the Chicago metropolitan area died after taking Tylenol capsules deliberately laced with cyanide.

Tylenol’s manufacturer, Johnson & Johnson, never learned who was responsible.

But rather than waiting for an answer that might never come, they recalled every capsule on shelves nationwide, redesigned the packaging to make tampering far harder and rebuilt public trust almost entirely.

In Johnson & Johnson’s case, adapting meant acting decisively on what they did know, instead of freezing in the face of what they didn't.

We’ve reached the end of our journey.

What have we learned?

Well, the lesson is to remember the 4 As: Assess, analyze, articulate and adapt.

Each skill depends on the one before it, and together they turn even the messiest situation into one you can actually do something about.

In this Blink to Visual Intelligence by Amy Herman, you've learned that sharpening how you see and communicate starts with four connected skills.

Assessing means observing deliberately, to compensate for the fact that your brain filters out far more than it registers.

Analyzing means checking your findings from every angle before trusting them, then deciding what matters most.

Articulating means choosing precise, objective words, since a vague or careless one can derail everything that follows.

And adapting means correcting for cognitive biases and acting decisively, even when a situation won’t resolve cleanly.

Together, these four skills will help you handle even the messiest situation with confidence.
